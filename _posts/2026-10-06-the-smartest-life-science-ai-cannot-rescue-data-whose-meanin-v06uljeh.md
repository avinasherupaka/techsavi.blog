> The smartest life-science AI cannot rescue data whose meaning changes from one laboratory to the next.

## The Perspective

The race to build more capable biological models is accelerating.

But model performance is only part of the system.

When assays use inconsistent definitions, provenance is incomplete, or quality controls appear late in the workflow, faster AI can simply produce inconsistent answers faster.

That makes scientific data standardization more than an IT project.

It becomes an experimental control.

The stronger operating model is a machine-readable laboratory: one designed so evidence can move from assay to analysis—and eventually toward regulatory use—without repeatedly reconstructing its meaning.

## Why It Matters

A practical scientific data contract can be built in five layers:

1. **Shared assay ontology** — Establish consistent definitions for what was measured and how.

2. **Machine-readable schema** — Capture results in structures that systems can interpret without manual translation.

3. **Provenance and versioning** — Preserve where data came from, what changed, and which version supported each decision.

4. **Automated quality checks** — Detect missing fields, invalid values, and structural inconsistencies close to capture.

5. **Export and portability tests** — Confirm that evidence can be reused across studies, tools, and submission workflows.

The goal is not rigid uniformity. Schemas should remain extensible, versioned, and governed by scientists who understand where variation is meaningful.

## Putting It Into Practice

Before investing in another AI layer, audit one critical assay workflow. Test whether its meaning, provenance, quality status, and transformations can be reconstructed without relying on tribal knowledge.
